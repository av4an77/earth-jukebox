The problem: Earth science is told only to the eye

NASA's Earth Information Center shows our planet through stunning visualizations. If you cannot see them, you get nothing.

  • About 2.2 billion people live with a vision impairment (World Health Organization).
  • Even for sighted people, a map is something you glance at. Music is something you feel and remember.
  • The challenge asks us to translate sight into sound, in real time, with accessibility at the core.

Our answer: a jukebox where every pixel plays a note

Pick a selection like A3 or B1. A needle sweeps the NASA picture from west to east. Each spot of data it crosses becomes a note, computed live in the browser. Nothing is pre-recorded.

Ten tracks, and data that updates itself

Earth Information Center

  • Sea surface currents and temperature (ECCO2)
  • Global carbon dioxide (GEOS DYAMOND)
  • Aerosols: dust, smoke, sulfate, sea salt (GEOS)
  • Lightning from the ISS (LIS)
  • Forest height (GEDI lidar)
  • Night lights change 2012–2023 (VIIRS)

Live from NASA satellites (GIBS)

  • Earth Right Now: the newest GOES-East image, refreshed as NASA publishes it
  • Sea surface temperature (GHRSST MUR) and rain and snow (GPM IMERG): newest, or any past day
  • Air temperature by month (MERRA-2): play a whole year and hear the seasons swing north and south

The page checks NASA for newer data every five minutes and changes the picture and the music by itself. Plus: drop in any image or video frame and hear it.

Built for people who cannot see the map

  • Explore mode: touch any spot, or move with the arrow keys, to hear it and have it described. On the rain map for 19 August 2025, moving to 25°N 72°W says “heavy rain, high”. That is Hurricane Erin.
  • Sound key: press a button (or keys 1 to 4) to hear each instrument and what it stands for, before the music starts.
  • Spoken captions say what you are hearing, and the music dips under the voice.
  • Guided tour plays every track with a spoken introduction. No sight or mouse needed.
  • Full keyboard control, screen-reader labels, visible focus, reduced-motion support, and phone vibration for lightning.
5 / 7

How it works, and what is honest about it

  • One static web page. HTML Canvas reads the pixels. The Web Audio API synthesizes every instrument. No server, no libraries, no samples.
  • Each frame is sampled on a 32 × 12 grid, keeping the most striking pixel in each cell so city lights and single lightning flashes are not averaged away.
  • Each dataset has its own color-to-value reader, checked against NASA's published color keys.
  • Live imagery comes straight from NASA GIBS. The page finds the newest time step that has data and keeps checking.
  • Code written with AI assistance (Anthropic Claude). All imagery is NASA's. No AI-generated images or audio.
  • Limit: values are estimated from the image colors, not the raw science files. Reading NetCDF data from NASA Earthdata is the next step, along with testing with blind and low-vision listeners.
